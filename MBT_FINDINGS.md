# Mini-Brain Toolkit: Implementation Findings

> V12, 2026-08-08.

Findings made while building the toolkit that are not evident from reading the files themselves. Each entry names the options considered and the reason the chosen approach won. Assume the current files are available as ground truth — this document does not restate what they already show.

---

**The namespace token choice carries a generalized lesson.** For a brain whose whole job is to sit beside other brains, the namespace token should be chosen for maximal distinctiveness under co-loading, not just for readability — the driver behind choosing `MBT` over blander alternatives like `BRAIN` or `TOOLKIT`.

**The two procedure docs are named `CREATE_BRAIN` and `CHECK_BRAIN`, not `ESTABLISH` / `ASSESS`.** The names moved twice toward obviousness: from precise verbs (`ESTABLISH`/`ASSESS`) to plain verbs (`CREATE`/`CHECK`) to verb-plus-object (`CREATE_BRAIN`/`CHECK_BRAIN`). The driver each time: the audience is an agent scanning a read index for the obvious action, and naming both the action and its object ("create a brain", "check a brain") is what a user actually types — matching the index entry to that intent lowers the chance of the wrong doc being loaded. Precision lost to obviousness on purpose; the fuller framing survives in each doc's body.

**The toolkit must stand on its own as a canonical source: cite external prior art, strip internal lineage.** References to outside research — the agent-memory landscape, ADRs, related systems — are signal and belong in the research agenda. References to *which mini-brain a convention came from*, or to how this toolkit applies the pattern to itself, are redundant and uninteresting to a reader learning the pattern — and they are a live bleed vector: an early version of the research agenda carried domain-specific examples (a batch of security-vendor acquisitions) verbatim from a sibling brain used as its seed template, content with no bearing on the mini-brain pattern. The rule that follows: distill conventions from exemplar brains, then cut the provenance — the canonical reference and procedure docs state the pattern on its own terms, and any build-time lineage worth keeping lives here in FINDINGS, which exists for exactly that.

**The work-closeout edit classification was preserved when generalizing from its exemplar, not stripped.** `templates/WORK_CLOSEOUT.md` classifies each piece of knowledge before merging it, the load-bearing distinction being reconcile-supersede vs. append-log. The exemplar carried this with platform-specific examples that invited deletion during generalization; it was kept because the value lives in that distinction, not the examples. The transferable caution: when distilling a template from one brain, judge a convention by its mechanics, not by whether its illustrations are project-specific.

**"Work item" replaced "feature" as the unit of tracked work.** The setup/closeout bookends and the per-item working set were originally named for *features*, but the machinery (the target walk, the reconcile/append-log classification, the branch→PR→merge→fold lifecycle) is agnostic to whether the work is a feature, a bug fix, or a hardening effort. Naming it "feature" undersold that reach and invited a reader to skip the ritual for non-feature work. Renaming to "work item" (files `WORK_SETUP`/`WORK_CLOSEOUT`, token `<WORK>`, dir `templates/work/`) widened the framing without changing the mechanics. Paired with the rename: setup now defaults to the **full scaffold** ("assume a branch and a PR until proven otherwise" — an oversized scaffold is cheap, under-scaffolding deep work is not), and closeout triggers on the work **concluding** (usually a merge, occasionally a no-code resolution) rather than strictly on merge.

**Commit conventions are deliberately not templated.** Both living brains grew a commit-conventions section in their entrypoints, and `templates/PROJECT_HOOK.md` defers to "the mini-brain's own commit conventions" — which reads from the outside like a template gap and was once proposed as one. It is an omission by design: how a repo commits is each brain's and each project's own choice, not a choice that is load-bearing for the mini-brain domain, so the templates stay silent and the hook's deferential wording binds a project repo to whatever its brain declares — including nothing. Living brains adding their own section is the system working, not template drift.

**Append-only was found to mean content integrity, not fixed position — no exception was carved.** Folding two logs into one during a restructuring looked blocked by the append-only rule: appending the retired log's entries after newer ones breaks chronology, interleaving them looked like a violation outright, and archiving is forbidden. The first response was to reject relaxing the rule, on the grounds that a migration-shaped exception weakens it for the common case to serve a rare one — sound reasoning, and unnecessary. The rule is stated in five places, and two of them already defined it as content integrity: an entry is never edited after the fact, and content above the new separator is never revised. Neither says anything about where a section may sit. Merging whole entries by date was therefore always legal, and only the bare phrase "append-only" suggested otherwise. Two alternatives lost to it: leaving the retired log in place as a closed sibling, which strands a document under a token no unit declares and makes every brain's checker carry a permanent exemption to serve a rare move; and folding it in as one summarized entry, which destroys the turn-by-turn lineage that session closeout exists to produce. Date merging also keeps the most recently written entry at the file's tail, which the read-from-the-last-separator ritual depends on. The rule that generalizes: when a standing rule appears to block a new move, read every place it is actually stated before carving an exception — the block may be an artifact of the rule's name rather than its content.

**The check procedure fails stop when the target isn't a mini-brain, rather than handing off to creation.** An early design let a failed "is this a brain?" precondition pivot into the create procedure — a helpful-seeming handoff. It was rejected on safety grounds: asking to *check* a directory presumes a brain already lives there, so pivoting to *create* is a state-changing action that could overwrite or damage whatever is actually there, the more so under auto-approval. The rule that generalizes: a failed precondition in one procedure is a reason to stop, report, and let the user confirm the path or intent — never to invoke a second procedure that changes state. Dropping the handoff also removed the only cross-procedure reference, so no maintenance-doc boundary carve-out was needed.
